Correction to yesterday’s edition
Yesterday’s briefing reported that Wall Street rallied more than 1% on Friday 4 September. That was wrong, and it inverted the call. Friday’s US session closed lower: the Dow fell 271.86 points (−0.51%) to 53,414.25, the S&P 500 slipped 0.38% to 7,718.60, and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 0.29% to 26,506.99. Those levels are corroborated by CNBC, TheStreet, the Washington Post index table and Schwab’s market update, and the mechanism is coherent: a hot payrolls print pushed Treasury yields up and equities down on revived rate-hike risk.

The figures printed yesterday (S&P 7,747.71, Dow 53,686.11, Nasdaq 26,584.06) appear to have come from an intraday snapshot rather than the close. The practical consequence: Monday does not open with an unpriced Wall Street rally to catch up to. The overall call below is revised accordingly, from constructive to cautious.

Overall Call

Cautious — sideways to mildly lower

The bull case that existed on paper yesterday does not survive the corrected US close. Indian cash closed Friday at 15:30 IST; US August payrolls printed at 18:00 IST at +162,000 against a consensus near 53,000, with unemployment steady at 4.1%. Wall Street’s verdict on that beat was negative — yields rose and all three majors closed down. So what India has left to absorb on Monday is not a rally but a modest risk-off session plus a firmer rate path.

The domestic tape was already weak before any of this. The Nifty finished Friday at 23,897.70, up a token 24 points (+0.10%), and the Sensex at 76,515.43, up 362.57 (+0.48%) — enough to snap a four-session losing streak but not to change the structure. On the week the Nifty fell about 1.1–1.15%, a fourth consecutive weekly decline, closing below both its 21-day and 55-day EMAs. A market below both those averages, four weeks down, does not get the benefit of the doubt.

Rates are the dominant variable and they point the wrong way. A labour market adding 162,000 jobs hands the hawks their argument three weeks before the 16 September FOMC. Post-payrolls, CME FedWatch showed a September hike at roughly 58%, and other pricing through the week ran between the low 50s and 60%+. A US hike cycle is a straightforward negative for emerging-market flows. The partial offset: dovish comments from Fed Governor Christopher Waller later Friday, plus a surging yen and a softer dollar, pulled hike odds back and sparked a late recovery that trimmed the day’s losses.

Crude is the second problem and it is geopolitical, not cyclical. Brent sits near $96, up close to 9% on the week, on renewed US–Iran exchanges around the Strait of Hormuz. For an economy importing the overwhelming majority of its crude, that is an inflation, current-account and margin story all at once, and it is the single clearest channel by which a foreign headline hits Indian earnings.

One genuine support, and it is domestic. The rupee strengthened roughly 1% last week to close near 94.48, helped by the RBI’s special FCNR(B) swap window, which closed early on 31 August after drawing more than $52 billion in NRI dollar inflows. A firmer rupee takes some of the sting out of $96 Brent and removes the currency-panic tail risk that usually accompanies an oil spike. Domestic institutions have also been absorbing foreign selling through the slide.

And a structural quirk: US markets are closed Monday for Labor Day. No Wall Street cue arrives during or after Monday’s Indian session and global liquidity will be thin. That argues against a trending move in either direction — nobody builds size into a holiday tape — and for a range that resolves only on Tuesday.

What flips the call. To bullish: the Nifty reclaiming and holding 24,000–24,100 with breadth behind it, FIIs turning net buyers, Brent slipping under $88 on any Hormuz de-escalation, the rupee extending gains through 94, or hike odds for 16 September fading back below 40% on further Waller-style dovish commentary. To bearish: a sustained break of 23,800, Brent decisively through $100, a fresh Gulf escalation headline during Indian hours, or hike odds firming toward 80%. Confirming sideways — the base case: a flat-to-soft open, crude holding $93–96 without new headlines, and volume fading through the afternoon into a shut US market.

The technical picture is the weak part. The Nifty closed below both its 21-day and 55-day EMAs, which alongside four consecutive down weeks describes a market in a corrective structure rather than a dip inside an uptrend. 23,800 is the level that matters on the downside; 24,000–24,100 is the reclaim that would start to change the story.

Sector dispersion last week was wide and thematically clean. Energy-linked sectors won on the crude spike while consumption and defensives lost:

  • Gainers: Nifty Oil & Gas +1.1%, Nifty Private Bank +0.3%, Nifty Energy +0.2%.
  • Losers: Nifty Auto −4%, Consumer Durables −2.6%, Media −2.3%, Healthcare −2.3%, FMCG −2%.

Auto down 4% in a week when crude is up 9% is the market pricing input costs and demand risk together. Adani Enterprises, Eicher Motors and Maruti Suzuki were among the notable weekly drags.

Crude Oil

Brent near $96, up close to 9% on the week — and the driver is entirely geopolitical. WTI is quoted near $91.7. The move is not a demand story: it is the resumption of US–Iran hostilities in and around the Strait of Hormuz for the first time in about a month, including US strikes on Iranian oil tankers and IRGC targets, Iranian attacks on commercial shipping, and reports of tankers striking naval mines.

The supply is still physically moving, which is why $96 is not $120. The US military escorted 40 commercial vessels carrying roughly 18 million barrels through the Strait in a single day — a wartime high. The market is pricing disruption risk, not disruption. That cuts both ways: any credible de-escalation deflates the premium quickly, and any successful closure of the Strait would move price far more than 9%.

For India this is the key transmission channel. Elevated crude feeds imported inflation, widens the current-account deficit, and compresses margins for autos, paints, tyres, aviation and chemicals while supporting upstream energy. The rupee’s 1% gain last week is the meaningful offset — it softens the landed cost of every barrel.

Gold

Gold around $4,430–4,480, lower on the payrolls print. Spot fell roughly 1.1% on Friday to about $4,429 after the strong jobs data lifted rate-hike bets; other feeds quote nearer $4,477 for the same session, so treat the level as approximate. Silver was quoted around $66.17.

Two forces are pulling in opposite directions and gold is caught between them. Higher-for-longer or higher-from-here US rates raise the opportunity cost of holding a non-yielding asset — bearish. An active shooting conflict at the world’s most important oil chokepoint is textbook safe-haven demand — bullish. The rate channel won last week; the geopolitical channel is the reason the sell-off was contained to about a percent.

Analyst ranges for September 2026 span roughly $4,136–$5,304, with J.P. Morgan looking for $6,000/oz by year-end and Deutsche Bank framing a run toward $5,000. Those are forecasts, not data, and the dispersion is the point — nobody has conviction here.

Major Events to Watch (India & USA)

  • FOMC, 16 September — the dominant event on the calendar. Market pricing after payrolls put a hike at roughly 58% (readings through the week ranged from the low 50s to above 60%), with the fed funds range moving to 3.50–3.75% if delivered. Every US data point between now and then is a hike-odds input.
  • Fed speakers — Governor Waller’s dovish remarks visibly moved odds on Friday. In a coin-flip meeting, individual commentary carries unusual weight; watch for it during the pre-blackout window.
  • Strait of Hormuz / US–Iran — the live geopolitical risk. Strikes and counter-strikes were still being reported into 5 September, including a US strike on an Iranian tanker in the Gulf of Oman. Headlines here land during Indian trading hours and are the most likely source of an intraday gap.
  • US markets closed Monday 7 September (Labor Day) — thin global liquidity, no overnight cue for Tuesday’s Indian open until the US reopens 8 September.
  • India: crude pass-through and inflation — with Brent near $96, upcoming CPI/WPI prints and any commentary on fuel pricing become materially more market-relevant than usual.
  • India: rupee and RBI — the FCNR(B) swap window closed early on 31 August after more than $52bn of inflows. Whether that support persists once the window’s effect fades is a live question for the currency.
